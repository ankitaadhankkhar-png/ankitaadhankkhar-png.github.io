About me

I wanted to design rooms. Now I think about how organisations design the way they use our data.

Law wasn't my first plan. As a teenager I wanted to be an interior designer, and law came along as the practical choice. It turned out to suit me far better than I expected. I was first drawn to family and criminal law, the most human side of legal practice.

Then came the Aadhaar case. As India's Supreme Court weighed the country's biometric ID programme against the right to privacy, I went down a rabbit hole of curiosity. One question led to the next: to Google Spain, the European case behind the "right to be forgotten", and from there into privacy law as a whole.

Over time my attention moved to a different kind of design: how organisations collect, use and protect information about people. Privacy by design, it turns out, is still design.

I kept noticing that the defining moments were happening in Europe first: the laws, the landmark cases, the debates. The GDPR and the AI Act were born here and have shaped laws far beyond the EU, and what caught my eye was their citizen-first approach. That is what brought me to Europe. Today I study data law on the Erasmus Mundus programme, and I write about what new rules mean in practice.

Off the clock, I'm still an interior designer at heart. I notice layouts everywhere, and I've been exploring European architecture between classes. I'm also learning German, one Artikel at a time, alongside my studies.
